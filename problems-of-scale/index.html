Problems of Scale

Fri, Oct 30, 2026 · Rob

Add the “scales falling from the eyes” Bible quote here It would make for a good “stop looking at the standardised tests” opening.

Little-e education has a scale problem. It always has. We start out taking care of and educating our own kids. When we don’t have the time/energy/patience/capacity the kids go to childcare (depending on how generous you are feeling and your own experiences, you might sometimes call this “early childhood education”). Kindergarten (confusingly swapped with “pre-primary” depending on which state you live in here in Australia) tends to have cozy class sizes, sometimes half days. From then on it’s a march into larger and larger classes until the winnowing out stages of late high school. From there students might be catapulted into lecture halls, or joining some arbitrary number of online learners.

Talk to teachers of large classes and I doubt you would find many that didn’t want smaller class sizes. Lower scale. Depending on the school they work in, this might be an issue of behaviour, or relationships, or just coping with the assessment load, but all of them boils down to scale.

Big-E Education also has a scale problem, although if you have an economics bent you might call it a productivity problem. How do systems understand the effectiveness of the messy process of little-e education? Of course the answer is standardisation; curriculum, teacher training, streaming, “like schools”, ICSEA, and of course tests, tests, tests. I think one of the problems here is that Big-E Education is not really education at all, it’s certification.

  • Are students allowed to progress to a particular course in senior secondary?
  • Do students achieve a certificate of graduation?
  • Can students enrol in a chosen university degree?
  • Are students allowed to attach some somewhat arbitrary letters to the end of their name?
The irony is that I like data

As long as you understand its limitations from its precision, bias, collection methodology, and all the other inconveniently varied human characterstics that we try to hide under cool, calm numbers. It’s quite funny (and wholly irrelevant to this piece) that after decades of presenting computers and algorithms as coldly rational machines that we’re dressing up massive matrices and pretending that they are conscious and feeling.

I find it interesting to think about the divergence of “education” from “training”. If students leave school and pursue an apprenticeship or a traineeship, the scale issue gets reduced. Certification is still there, but it is more of a mentorship model: relationships, observation, moving forward when the subject is ready, less reliance on numbers telling a story alone.

I have little faith in the trajectory of AI in education as an answer to the scale problem. It relies too much on student agency, inhuman judgements of context, and invasive data collection by companies that are motivated by profit and not what is good for people or humanity in general. It may very well be transformative for general productivity, and maybe there is where we can start paying attention to litt-e-e education again. Make education more about relationships, shared understanding, collaboration, and being human again.

My concern is in order to make education not about certification anymore you would need to burn down the whole system. Scrap curriculum that mandates rigid progression, scrap standardised testing that obsesses over whether you have progressed “enough” this year, scrap university entrance exams that are driven by business pressure. Make education small. Incentivise personal progress and development without the need for comparison with anyone other than yourself.

However… how do you get enough educators to be able to achieve this without the scale problems?
